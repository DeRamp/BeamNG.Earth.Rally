a stage never driven, telling you what it is: 16 km, 82 corners the co-driver will call, 550 m up and 220 m back down. none of this needs a run, a world, or a car.

Why 82 is allowed to be on that tile. the owner's instruction was "base corners off the co-driver", and 82 is not an approximation of what the co-driver calls — it is the same count, from the same function. cornersFound is not the game's number at all: the mod finds the corners itself, geo.corners (geo.lua:184-236), writes one pacenote per corner (stageWriter.lua:127-199), and the engine then measures each one's severity and calls it. the stored runs show the two agreeing: on the matched line, cornersFound 31 and notesLoaded 32 — 31 corner notes plus one junction note standing on its own — and notesCalled 32. the number on the tile is the number of corner calls, by construction.

And it needs nothing from the game. geo.lua says so in its first line ("plain geometry on stage lines, with nothing from the game"), geo.corners reads only .x and .y, and the frame that turns the line's lat / lon into metres is the mod's own (autoRecce.lua:108-114, "the stage's own frame, not the game's"). so the pass can run the moment a line is saved — at the end of a recce, and at the end of an auto recce — and for lines saved by older versions, once, on the next look. it must be kept, not recomputed on sight: the stage page re-asks uiStage every two seconds while it is open (EarthRally.vue:280-288), and the longest line in the set is 9 785 samples — a pass to run on save, not on a poll.

5 · units: one setting, no second way of doing it

the Length tile reads lenNum(stg.lengthM) with units() in its <small>, and units() answers "km" or "mi" from the one existing setting (store.js). the new tiles follow it with no new setting and no new switch: metric gives metres, imperial gives feet — which is exactly the pairing the HUD's own format.js dist() already makes (× 3.28084, feet for the imperial short distance).

6 · the numbers behind the threshold

summing every step of a 4 m-spaced line adds the height data's own wobble to the road's real rise. the threshold is a hysteresis: a move is only counted once it has gone that far from the last counted height, so a 1 m shiver on a flat road is dropped and a 1 m rise in the middle of a long climb still arrives in the total. every column below is measured over the user's own 116 matched lines.

flat stage: Founders Olmstead, Moore, Nagle Super Special, 5.8 km, every point inside a 3.2 m band of height — whatever its true climb is, it is a few metres, so the column is very nearly all error. mirror: Dalles Mtn Up against Dalles Mtn Down, two separate lines of the same road, how far the one's climb is from the other's drop. resampled: the same line measured again at 20 m spacing instead of 4 m, as a percentage of the 4 m figure — a method reading the road gives the same answer at either spacing, a method reading the wobble does not. net: how far climb minus drop falls from the line's own end-to-start height change, median.

2 m, and why not further. the flat stage falls 23 → 7 → 2 m over the first two metres of threshold and then has nothing left to give: that is where the wobble ends. past 2 m the figure keeps falling by about 1.3 m/km for each further metre of threshold, and now it is taking real rises with it — a 3 m roll over 60 m of road is a crest a driver feels, and at a 5 m threshold it is gone. 2 m is also the only row that is both exactly stable when the line is resampled at 20 m and inside half a percent on the mirror, and it keeps climb minus drop within a metre of the line's own net height change, so the two tiles cannot quietly disagree with each other.

Smoothing is the worse tool here. a 60 m moving average leaves 0 m on the flat stage too, but it reads 108% of itself when the line is resampled and it flattens the short rises along with the noise. the threshold throws away small moves; smoothing shrinks every move, including the big ones.

Maryhill Up by both methods, which is the one comparison that can be done like for like. the same stage exists as a python-matched line and as a line the in-game auto recce wrote. corners: 31 and 31, identical. climb: 185 m against 238 m, and drop 0 m against 44 m. the python line's heights come from the elevation data sampled every 4 m and climb steadily; the in-game line's come from road-graph node heights interpolated between nodes (autoRecce.lua:554-583), and they contain about 25 descending stretches on a road that never descends — the worst drops 11 m in 36 m, a 30% gradient that is not there. no threshold under 10 m removes them, and at 10 m the two lines agree (182 against 195) at the cost of every real crest in the set. Dalles Mtn Up, the other stage that exists both ways, agrees well — 548 / 223 python against 545 / 219 in game, 0.5% and 2% — so this is not a flaw in the in-game pass as such, it is some of that road's node heights being wrong. a line a driver has driven is the third kind and the cleanest: the recce of Maryhill Up gives 182 / 0, within 2% of the python line.

So the honest statement is that the climb figure carries the accuracy of whichever line the stage has, and on one of the two stages where we can check, the in-game line overstates the climb by about a quarter. that is a road-graph height problem to fix where the heights are read, not something a summing rule should hide: pushing the threshold to 10 m to make Maryhill agree would cost the whole set its real small climbs, which is the same trade the owner refused on corners.

7 · what this drawing settles, and what it does not

Settled by drawing it. the four tiles become Length · Corners · Climb · Drop with no change to .er-tiles, .er-tile, .er-v or .er-l — two labels and two sources. Corners has exactly four states and only one dash, and that dash means "no line", the one state the number genuinely cannot be had in. there is no unknown state for a recced-but-undriven stage, because the co-driver's own corner pass is pure geometry on the line and the count can be had the moment the line is saved. the new tiles take their unit from units() and add no setting.

Not settled, and wanting a decision. (1) the words. Climb and Drop are drawn because they are short enough for the 14 px label and they are what a driver says; Gain / Loss is what the owner wrote in the report, Up / Down is shortest, Ascent / Descent is what a map would print. (2) the rounding. nearest 10 m and nearest 50 ft are drawn; format.js dist() rounds feet to the nearest 10, and following it exactly would make the flat stage read 0 m in metric and 10 ft in imperial, which is the sort of small lie that is worth one decision now. (3) whether Junctions really goes. it is the user's call and this drawing assumes it; if it stays, Drop is the tile with no room and the page needs five. (4) whether a stage with a silent stretch should mark its Corners number at all — drawn plain, on the grounds that the pill beside it already says the stage has one. (5) where the kept figure lives: beside the line in its own file, or in the events index. not a UI question, but it decides whether an old line gets its number on the next look or on its next save.

Not verified. no browser was opened: the 980 px column, the 233 px tile and the widths of the numbers in it are arithmetic on EarthRally.vue's own values, with Overpass Mono taken at about .6em an advance, so a tile said to have room over has room over by arithmetic. the corner counts are a python port of geo.corners and its callers, checked against the figures the game itself recorded — exact on the matched lines (31 and 31), one out on the recce lines (27 against the stored 28, 18 against 19), which is almost certainly the start and finish controls' own placement along a recce line (runner.lua:204-215) and not the detector; a stage's true figure should be confirmed from the game once before these tiles are believed to the unit. the climb figures are not checked against anything outside the line files themselves: there is no survey here, so "believable" means the four tests in section 6 and nothing stronger. and nothing in this file has been rendered by the game's own stylesheet — only by this copy of it.
